Who took part and how it went

Of 408 records, 14 had no history matrix answers, one recorded age 30 and three were excluded on response quality flags. All other ages were retained. The three flags were two uniform response patterns and one rapid response with a uniform block and minimal explanation.

Completion time

14 min 38 sec median among 381 students who reached the difficulty item. The raw mean was 17 min 54 sec and the range was 4 min 46 sec to 6 h 51 min. Excluding three sessions above one hour gives a mean of 16 min 14 sec.

Perceived difficulty

54.3% easy, 42.8% neutral and 2.9% difficult. The 381 students who answered this question provide the denominator. Open comments identify comprehension problems even among some students who found the survey manageable.

03 · Measurement

Five provisional scales

Each score is the mean of its component items on a 1–5 scale, requiring all items. Agreement and frequency scales use different anchors. Teaching scores describe students’ reported opportunities. Evidence-based beliefs describe endorsement of epistemic principles.

Internal consistency and scale descriptives
ScaleItemsNMeanSDα95% interval

Alpha intervals use 4,000 student bootstrap resamples. They describe uncertainty under individual resampling, with no adjustment for school clustering. Corrected item–total correlations use complete scale records. Response counts and missingness use all retained students in the selected group.

How the provisional structure was selected

Blockwise PCA and parallel analysis informed the scoring choices. The Q13 meaning block had a borderline second component and the three proposed distance items had weak internal consistency (α = .300). Q16 supported two components, separating classroom practices from visits and organised remembrance. Q17 supported one component. The two commemoration items correlate at .627 (Spearman–Brown = .771). A joint ordinal factor model and independent confirmation remain pending.

04 · Substantive findings

Classroom experience, meaning and remembrance

Teaching and perceived meaning

Meaning correlates with enquiry teaching (r = .459) and democratic and inclusive teaching (r = .496). After removing school mean differences, the associations are .410 and .435. The two teaching scales correlate at .607 pooled and .576 within schools.

Religion and commemoration

The pooled correlation is .245 (n = 369). It falls to .081 after removing country mean differences and .052 after removing school mean differences. The centring analysis is descriptive.

The dictator statue task

386 students selected an option. Moving the statue to a museum was the most common response. The comparison across the five pilot samples yielded χ²(12) = 16.72, p = .160, Cramér’s V = .120.

How students justified each choice

We grouped written explanations by the option selected. The branch totals include brief answers and answers that repeat the option wording. The themes below illustrate different arguments within each branch. The examples paraphrase student responses.

Keep it in the same place · 60 students

Students invoked heritage, the original location, the leader’s achievements or a duty to remember a difficult past. One student argued that moving the statue would diminish its historical meaning. Another feared that forgetting the dictator would make a return to dictatorship more likely. Others believed the leader deserved recognition.

Keep it and add information · 118 students

Some students wanted a panel to explain the leader’s actions and acknowledge victims. Others wanted visitors to consider competing perspectives or learn more about the leader’s life. Some saw this as a compromise. One student made retention conditional on the families’ agreement, giving them a role in the decision.

Move it to a museum · 175 students

Students proposed preserving the object for teaching, research or protection from damage. Some saw relocation as a way to end a public tribute while keeping the object available for critical interpretation. Others stressed voluntary access. People who wished to see it could visit, while families and residents could avoid an unwanted daily reminder.

Remove and destroy it · 33 students

Students invoked respect for victims and families, rejection of dictatorship and the wish to withdraw recognition altogether. One student proposed honouring those who died. Several explained why the statue should come down while leaving their reason for destroying the object implicit.

Preserving an object and conferring public honour

Students who supported a plaque and students who supported a museum could both want the history taught and victims remembered. They differed in what they expected the setting to communicate. Some believed an explanation could change the statue’s meaning in the square. Others saw the prominent location itself as an expression of civic honour.

Research context and interpretation

Seixas and Clark distinguish three moments in students’ reasoning about contested monuments: the past depicted, the later act of representing it, and the present decision about the representation. In this pilot, a student can condemn a regime, value the statue as evidence of how the regime was commemorated, and question where it should stand. Seixas and Clark, Murals as Monuments (2004).

Research on contested heritage frames contextualisation as an attempt to change what a display communicates. The prominence of the object, the interpretation provided and who has a say all matter. Museum research in Santiago and Belfast also points to different effects across exhibitions and settings. Burch-Brown (2022), Balcells, Palanza and Voytas (2022) and Balcells and Voytas (2026).

The handling options are nominal. The explanations show how students link the past to present choices and future consequences. They also help distinguish judgements about the regime from judgements about the statue, its location and its audience. The themes come from an exploratory qualitative reading. The prompt itself supplied reasons for some options, and some students inferred a specific leader or national setting.

Past, present and future

Of 386 students with both answers, 196 chose a past focus and 165 chose all three time horizons. The offered purposes of history and time orientations were associated (Cramér’s V = .170). These nominal choices can guide analysis of students’ reasoning without assigning them to developmental levels.

05 · Response processes

What students told us

123 students entered something in the final comment box, including brief acknowledgements and punctuation. The examples below paraphrase voluntary comments and illustrate issues for questionnaire revision and teaching.

Which teacher should I think about?

A student in Spain described very different practices in consecutive years and was unsure which teacher the questions referred to. The next version needs an explicit reference period.

How often is “often”?

An Irish student wondered whether the label meant weekly or monthly. Students in Luxembourg and Cyprus also identified long sentences or difficult vocabulary.

Studying for the exam

Some students contrasted discussion, varied materials and excursions with studying to reproduce textbook or teacher accounts. Several called for visits and stronger links to current events.

Different ideas of relevance

Some asked for more local or national history. Others wanted more attention to Roma and Travellers, racism or persecution. One student interpreted some items as signalling a preferred political answer.

06 · Revision priorities

What to do before the main study

  1. Revise and cognitively test the instrument. Agree a reference period, clarify frequency and unfamiliar content, document translation decisions and ask students to explain their interpretation of selected items.
  2. Refine scoring. Review the weak meaning scale in Spain at item level. Confirm the five-scale structure on new data with ordinal models and add model-based reliability estimates where supported. Keep personal irrelevance as a separate item pending development.
  3. Gather evidence for historical thinking. Add short source interpretation or corroboration tasks. Develop and independently double-code a rubric for the reasoning in the statue explanations.
  4. Design for cross-country comparison. Recruit sufficient schools and students per country and language version, then assess comparable factor structure, loadings and thresholds using appropriately identified models. Examine item functioning before making comparative score claims.
  5. Specify interpretation and reporting. Document what each score can support, sample coverage, exclusions, sensitivity checks and how students’ feedback informed revisions.
